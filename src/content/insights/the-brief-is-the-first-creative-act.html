Most conversations about advertising start with the idea. The better ones start earlier — with the brief. A brief is not paperwork that comes before the creative work. It is the first creative decision, and it shapes everything that follows.

Everything, therefore nothing

The most common brief we see asks a single campaign to build awareness, drive footfall, launch a product, reposition the brand and speak to three audiences at once. Every one of those objectives is reasonable. Together, they make it almost impossible to say anything clearly.

Advertising works by leaving things out. A campaign that tries to say five things gets remembered for none of them. The job of the brief is to make the hard choice before the creative team does — so they can spend their energy on how to say it, not on what to say.

What a useful brief contains

A useful brief is short. It usually fits on a single page and answers five questions honestly:

  • What is the business problem? Not the communication task — the commercial reason this work exists.
  • Who are we talking to? One audience, described as people rather than demographics.
  • What do they think now? The belief or behaviour we need to change.
  • What do we want them to think or do instead? One shift, stated simply.
  • Why should they believe us? The proof, product truth or insight that makes the shift credible.

If any of these answers is vague, the work will be too.

Insight is not a fact

“Customers want quality at a fair price” is a fact, and every competitor can claim it. An insight is a truth about people that makes an audience feel understood and gives the brand a reason to say something no one else is saying. It is usually uncomfortable, specific and slightly surprising.

Finding it takes time: talking to customers, listening to the sales team, reading reviews, watching how people actually buy. That time is the best investment in the whole process, because a sharp insight makes the creative work faster and the media spend more efficient.

Write it together

The best briefs are not handed over; they are built together. When the client team and the agency agree on the problem before anyone starts designing, feedback becomes simpler. The question stops being “do we like it?” and becomes “does it solve the problem we agreed on?”

That shift changes the quality of the work more than any single idea can. Good advertising starts with a good idea. A good idea starts with a good brief.
